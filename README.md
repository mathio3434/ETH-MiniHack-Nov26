# ETH-MiniHack-Nov26 by Optiver

# Background
On Friday 6th November, I had the opportunity to visit the Mini Hackathon by Optiver and the ETH Entrepreneurship Club in Zurich. Before the event, I spent the week doing my best to understand the basics of market making. In this repository, I would like to elaborate on my experience during the event, without directly giving any hints, to ensure fair competition at future hackathons.

# Challenge Structure
The hackathon lasted around 5 hours of testing time plus one final evaluation round, during which the winner was determined. During the preparation round, we tested different strategies and ideas. We traded a universe of 6 tech stocks (no options, ETFs or futures). What mattered was not the fundamentals (the prices were not a representation of a real market price) but the patterns and behaviour of other participants. Teams of 3-4 members were formed and given access to a cloud kernel to run research and ideas using Optibook. (You can find further information online, as I did before the competition; the difference is that every challenge differs in its structure and limitations.)

# Elaboration on my team's final model
Before the challenge, I drafted a [template version](https://github.com/mathio3434/ETH-MiniHack-Nov26/blob/main/template) of the market-making bot using the basics from [Marco Avellaneda & Sasha Stoikov](https://people.orie.cornell.edu/sfs33/LimitOrderBook.pdf), introducing risk-aversion and sensitivity parameters to control the reservation price and volatility spreads. Additionally, I implemented order imbalance to control inventory and adjust the quoting (if I build a positive inventory, I quote asks closer to my reservation price). Volatility spreads were built using a half-life EWMA to ensure a quick reaction to sudden volatility changes (my favourite metric for short-horizon volatility measurement). The most important part is, of course, the limits on different sides, some implemented as mechanical constraints (e.g., our bid cannot be higher than our ask, or lower than a specific "untraded" bound) and some as controls (e.g., sigma multiplier or order imbalance).

**Equation to define BID / ASK**:

$$P_{Bid} = MP + OI - INVST - 1/2(HF+VA)$$

$$P_{Ask} = MP + OI - INVST + 1/2(HF+VA)$$

where: MP - Micro Price, OI - Order Book Imbalance, INVST - Inventory Shift (tanh limit [-1,1]), HF - Half Spread, VA - Volatility Adjustment. For simplicity, I have left out all the limits and specific formulas; you can find them in the [template version](https://github.com/mathio3434/ETH-MiniHack-Nov26/blob/main/template). Additionally, the model has a robust, conservative infrastructure for order handling: it ensures that all limits are respected, cleans up outstanding orders, handles the case of another user connecting, provides a manual hand-brake option, and manages orders clearly. One pronounced trade-off is that if additional orders are possible, the old order is deleted before the new one is inserted. If we hit exchange call limits, this can result in price/time slippage. However, the code ensures that orders are placed up to the desired magnitude, conditional on free outstanding limits, including a possible fill of all orders. Since the infrastructure is rather general (except for the limits), I do not see any comparative advantage in the model unless it is calibrated for specific markets, which leaves it with a central educational role.

**Final model**
In the final 2 hours of the competition, we could finally get our hands on the data for the idea we thought might be our edge. The core of what we found was a lagged relationship between variables. In other words, we found that the universe of 3 assets (A, B, C, D) exhibits the following relationships:
1. *A* holds a constant price relationship to *B* over time. If the ratio deviates, it statistically corrects during the next 30s bar. In that regard, if the price of one asset changes its value, we would revise $Expected Price_{A} = New Price_{B} / Ratio$. If the current price deviates, we considered two possible strategies (assume the current price is lower than expected by 500 ticks):
   a. Aggressive: buy the asks up to x ticks away from the 500, anticipating the jump. This strategy is about market taking, and it is what we decided to do: our environment was driven by sharp P&L jumps, so to survive we had to be aggressive.
   b. Passive: a market-making approach would simply shift quotes to adjust to the fair price. We would want higher bids so as to be filled cheaply. This conservative view went stale in the trading environment that we had.
2. *C* had a 0.96 correlation with *D*, though the co-movement magnitude (*beta*) is 0.6, meaning a 1% move in C should on average be a 0.6% move in D. In practice, 60% of the gap closed within about 20s. We decided on a strategy that establishes the next 20s reservation price based on this relationship, and we move our quotes toward that price up to a limit of x (if the price is higher, buy out the asks), but only so far as we keep a fair position and competitive quotes centred around that price.

I would like to comment that this was a conceptual idea that did not work out in the environment where it was applied. Maybe it is a great example of resulting: we thought we had a good hand that turned out badly (to paraphrase Annie Duke), or maybe we overfitted our signal to a distorted market environment, since this relationship could be a byproduct of the poor quoting models of other participants, which some could capture and we could not: we traded the crumbs while the other guys took the pie.

# Lessons learned from the challenge
1. **Figure out how the system works** - this sounds very plain, but it took us a good 30 minutes, which is significant. The problem was that although the environment was in the cloud and all team members had access, only one person could run a bot, a history or a test at a time. This was crucial for the next point.
2. **Figure out what needs to be done when** - this sounds very simple as well, but it is how it works, and under stress people tend to rush and do everything at once. In our case, it was crucial to figure out how to get the history of data and build upon a few rounds of information (how do others quote? what are the asset relationships?). Unfortunately, we only managed this close to the end of the competition, so we built our strategy more from discretionary observation than from statistics.
3. **Test, test, test** - regardless of the state of your bot or idea, the whole approach is Bayesian: build, get feedback, implement, repeat. I think this is what we did best. Before the competition, I had a classic, "soulless" market-making model, with which we could start from the very beginning and already gather some feedback about the environment we were trading in. This gave us more ideas, from lag trading to volatility spread expansion, which, although they did not result in an astonishing payoff, were still a stable, positive-EV strategy. The fact that we were "in action" not only motivated us to think about a problem of unknown nature, but also helped us set foot in that unknown nature to adapt and grow. As the Nike slogan says: "Just do it!"
4. **There is no ready formula for success** - and I mean this in a very broad sense. Before jumping into the competition, I read some papers and constructed pseudo-code for the quoting mechanics from those papers: microprice, order book imbalance, volatility adjustments... I was very convinced that I had figured it all out, but I had not. It is not only that I treated some aspects of market making in a very simplified way; I also thought that if I took a cheat sheet from Calculus 1, I could pass an exam on differential equations. Of course, it just does not work this way. The main point of the challenge is to understand the machine you are driving, where you are driving it, and with whom you are driving it. So, without further thinking:
5. **Do not take all of those reasons as advice - figure everything out yourself.** Trading is not about being right, but about acknowledging when you are wrong and adjusting:                                          *Keep your mind open and your hands ready for bayesian updating*.
