# ETH-MiniHack-Nov26 by Optiver

# Background
On Friday, 6th November, I had the opportunity to attend the Mini Hackathon by Optiver and the ETH Entrepreneurship Club in Zurich. I spent the week before the event trying to understand the basics of market making. In this repository I describe my experience during the event, without giving direct hints, to keep future hackathons fair.

# Challenge Structure
The hackathon consisted of about 5 hours of testing time and one final evaluation round, during which the winner was determined. During the preparation phase, we tested different strategies and ideas. We traded a universe of 6 tech stocks (no options, ETFs or futures). Fundamentals did not matter, as prices did not represent real market prices, what mattered were the patterns and behaviour of the other participants. Teams of 3-4 members were formed and given access to a cloud kernel to run research and ideas using Optibook. (You can find further information online, as I did before the competition; every challenge differs in its structure and limitations.)

# Elaboration on the final model of my team
Before the challenge I drafted a [template version](https://github.com/mathio3434/ETH-MiniHack-Nov26/blob/main/template) of a market-making bot, based on the model of [Marco Avellaneda & Sasha Stoikov](https://people.orie.cornell.edu/sfs33/LimitOrderBook.pdf), with risk-aversion and sensitivity parameters to control the reservation price and the volatility spread. I also added order book imbalance as a signal to adjust the quoting, and an inventory shift to control the position I hold (e.g. if I build up a positive inventory, I shift my reservation price down so that my asks become more competitive and I can sell the inventory down). The volatility spread is built from an EWMA with a half-life, which reacts quickly to sudden changes in volatility (my favourite metric for short-horizon volatility).

The most important part is, of course, the limits on each side. Some are mechanical (e.g. our bid cannot be higher than our ask, or lower than a specific "untraded" bound), and others act as controls (e.g. the sigma multiplier or the order book imbalance).

**Equation to define BID / ASK**:

$$P_{Bid} = MP + OI - INVST - 1/2(HF+VA)$$

$$P_{Ask} = MP + OI - INVST + 1/2(HF+VA)$$

where: MP - microprice, OI - order book imbalance, INVST - inventory shift (tanh-bounded to [-1, 1]), HF - base spread (*2), VA - volatility adjustment. For simplicity I have left out all the limits and specific formulas; you can find them in the [template version](https://github.com/mathio3434/ETH-MiniHack-Nov26/blob/main/template).

The model also has a robust, conservative infrastructure for order handling: it keeps within all limits, cleans up outstanding orders, reacts if another user connects, has a manual emergency stop, and manages orders clearly. One notable trade-off is that if additional orders are possible, the existing order is deleted before the new one is inserted. If we run into the exchange call limit, this can cost us price/time priority. However, the code places orders up to the desired size, subject to the free outstanding limits, including the possible fill of all resting orders. Since the infrastructure is rather general (except for the limits), I do not see any comparative advantage in the model unless it is calibrated to the specific market, so its role is mainly educational.

**Final model**
In the final 2 hours of the competition we finally got our hands on the data to test the idea we thought might be our edge. The core of what we found was a lagged relationship between variables. In other words, we found that the universe of 4 assets (A, B, C, D) exhibits the following relationships:
1. *A* holds a roughly constant price ratio to *B* over time (Ratio = Price_B / Price_A). If the ratio deviates, it statistically corrects within the next bar. So whenever the price of one asset changes, we revise the expected price: $Expected Price_{A} = Price_{B} / Ratio$. If the current price deviates, we considered two possible strategies:
   a. Aggressive: buy the asks as long as the price is still more than x ticks below the expected price, anticipating the convergence. This is market taking, and it is what we decided to do: our environment was driven by sharp price jumps, so to survive we had to be aggressive.
   b. Passive: a market maker would simply shift the quotes to the new fair price, i.e. quote higher bids to get filled at a cheap price. This is the conservative view, and in our trading environment it turned out to be too passive.
2. *C* has a significant correlation with *D*, but the co-movement magnitude (beta) is b, meaning that a n% move in C should on average be a bn% move in D. In practice, about k% of the gap closed within next bar. We set the reservation price for the next bar based on this relationship, and we take quotes on the price side up to a limit of x ticks (if the fair price is higher than the market, we buy the asks), but only as far as we keep a fair position and competitive quotes centred around that price.

I would like to comment that this was a conceptual idea that did not work out in the environment where it was applied. Maybe it is a good example of "resulting" (a rephrasing of Annie Duke): we thought we had a good hand that turned out badly. Or maybe we overfitted our signal to a distorted market environment, since this relationship could be a by-product of the poor quoting models of other participants, which some teams could capture and we could not: we traded the crumbs while others took the pie.

# Lessons learned from the challenge
1. **Figure out how the system works** - it sounds plain, but it took us a full 50 minutes, which is significant at a scale of 5 hours. Although the environment was in the cloud and all team members had access, only one person could run a bot, a history query or a test at a time. This was crucial for the next point.
2. **Figure out what needs to be done and when** - this sounds obvious as well, but under stress people tend to do everything at once. In our case, it was crucial to figure out early how to get the historical data and build on a few rounds of information (how do the others quote? what are the relationships between assets?). Unfortunately, we only did this close to the end of the competition, so we built our strategy more on discretionary observation than on statistics.
3. **Test, test, test** - regardless of the state of your bot or idea, the whole process is Bayesian in spirit: build, get feedback, implement, repeat. I think this is what we did best. Before the competition I had a classic, "soulless" market-making model, so we could start from the very beginning and already gather feedback about the environment we were trading in. This gave us more ideas, from lag trading to volatility-spread expansion, which did not produce an astonishing payoff but was still a stable, positive-EV strategy. Being "in action" not only motivated us to think about a problem of unknown nature, but also helped us get a foot into that unknown, adapt and grow. As the Nike slogan says: "Just do it!"
4. **There is no ready formula for success** - and I mean this in a very broad sense. Before the competition I read some papers and wrote pseudo-code for the quoting mechanics from them: microprice, order book imbalance, volatility adjustments... I was convinced that I had it all figured out, but I did not. It is not only that I implemented several aspects of market making in a very simplified way; I also thought that taking a Calculus 1 cheat sheet would let me pass an exam on differential equations. Of course, it does not work this way. The main point of the challenge is to understand the machine you are driving, where you are driving it and with whom. So, without further ado:
5. **Do not take all of these lessons as advice - figure everything out yourself.** Trading is not about being right, but about acknowledging when you are wrong and adjusting: *Keep your mind clear and ready for bayesian updating!*
