# ETH-MiniHack-Nov26 by Optiver

# Background
On Friday 6th November, I had an opportunity to visit the Mini Hackathon by Optiver & ETH Entrepreneurship CLub in Zurich. Before the event, I have spent the week doing my best to understand the basics of the market-making and in this repository I would like to elaborate on my challenges during the event, while not directly giving any hints to ensure the fair competition for the next hackathons.

# Challenge Structure
The hackathon took place for around 5 hours of testing time and one final evaluation round, during which the winner was estimated. During the preparation round, we were testing dfferent stratagies and ideas. We were trading a universe of 6 Tech stocks (no options, etfs or futures), although it is not the fundamentals that mattered behind (as prices were not a representation of a real market price), but the patterns and behaviour of other participants. The teams of 3-4 members were formed and given access to a cloud kernel to run the research and ideas using Optibook. (You can find any further information online, as I did before the competition, difference is, every challenge differes in its structure and limitations)

# Lessons learned from the challenge
1. Figure out how the system works - sounds very plain, but in fact, it is what took us great 30 mins, which is a significant time. Problem was that although the environment was in a cloud and all team members had an access, it was always only one person running a bot or a history or a test. This was crucial for the next point.
2. Figure out what is need to be done when - sounds very stupid as well, but it is how it works and under stress a person tends to do all at once as a rush. In our case, it was crucial to figure out how to get the history of data and build upon few rounds of information (how do others quote? what are asset relationships?). Unfortunately, we could only do it close to the end of competition, buidling our strategy more from discretionary observation side rather than statistical.
3. Test, test, test - regardless of the state of your bot or idea, the whole idea is Bayesian: build, get feedback, implement, repeat. This is what I think we have done best. Before the competition I have had a classic, "soulless" market making model, which we could start from whole beginnig and already gather some feedback about the environment we were trading in. This is what gave us more ideas, from lag trading to volatility spreads expansion, which although did not result into astonishing payoff, was still a stable, positive EV strategy. The fact that we were "in action" not only motivated us to think on a problem of unkown nature, but also helped us set a foot into that unkown nature to adapt & grow. As Nike slogan says: "Just do it!"
4. There is no ready formula for success - and I mean it in a very broad sense. Before jumping into the competition, I have read some papers, constructed pseudo-code on the quoting mechanics from those paper: microprice, order book imbalance, volatility adjustments... I was very convinced that I have figured it all, but in fact no. It is not only about the aspects of market making that I was doing very simplified, but also I just though that if I take a cheet sheet in Calculus 1 than I can pass an exam on differential equations, of course it jsut does not work this way. A main thing about the challenge is to understand the machine you are driving, where are you driving it and with whom are your driving it. So without further thinking:
5. Do not listen to all of those reasons as advice - figure everything yourself. Trading is not about being right, but about acknowledging when you are wrong and adjusting: keep your mind open and your hands ready for Bayesian Updating.
